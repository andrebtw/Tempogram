Click interval timer

Click or tap the red area (or press Space) on each beat. Statistics and charts update after every click.

These numbers have no medical value

Everything on this page comes from you clicking along with a pulse you feel. Your reaction time, a missed or double click, losing count, or beats too faint to feel all change the results. The statistics, charts and comments are a rough illustration of your clicks, not a measurement of your heart.

Never use this page to decide whether you are safe, whether something is wrong, or whether to take more of anything. If you feel unwell, trust how you feel over any number here.

What the numbers suggest

An automatic reading of your statistics against common reference ranges. It is a guide, not a diagnosis.

Reminder: these comments are based on hand clicks, not a sensor, and can't confirm or rule out anything.

    Call emergency services (112 in Europe, or your local number) if there is chest pain or tightness, fainting, seizures, a very high body temperature, severe breathlessness, confusion, or if someone is very drowsy, can't be woken, or is breathing slowly or with gurgling or snoring sounds. If opioids might be involved, give naloxone if available. Put an unconscious person who is breathing in the recovery position and stay with them. Tell responders what was taken. Don't wait for a reading from this page. Harm reduction info: tripsit.me, dancesafe.org, psychonautwiki.org.

    What do these values mean?
    Range
    Longest interval minus shortest interval.
    Standard deviation (SD) and variance
    How far intervals typically sit from the mean. Sample formula (divides by n − 1). Variance is SD squared. In heart-rate research the SD of all intervals is called SDNN.
    CV
    Coefficient of variation: SD divided by the mean, as a percentage. It lets you compare variability between people or heart rates.
    IQR
    Interquartile range: the spread of the middle 50% of intervals. Less affected by one odd beat than the range.
    BPM
    60 000 divided by the interval in ms. Mean BPM uses the mean interval. The slowest beat is the longest interval, the fastest is the shortest.
    RMSSD
    Root mean square of successive differences. The most common short-term heart-rate-variability measure.
    SDSD
    Standard deviation of the successive differences.
    pNN50
    Share of successive intervals that differ by more than 50 ms.
    SD1 and SD2
    Width (SD1, short-term) and length (SD2, long-term) of the Poincaré ellipse.

    Measuring by hand clicks adds reaction-time noise of roughly 20–50 ms, so variability values (especially RMSSD and pNN50) will read higher than a real ECG or chest-strap would give. Use this for relative comparisons, not diagnosis.
